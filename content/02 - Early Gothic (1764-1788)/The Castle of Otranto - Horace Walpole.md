## Synopsis
Walpole presents the Castle of Otranto as an English translation of a recently discovered manuscript. The first edition’s preface suggests the manuscript was written between 1095 and 1243, or shortly after, and printed in Naples in 1529. The manuscript tells the story of Manfred, prince of Otranto. At the start, Manfred waits impatiently for the marriage of his sickly son Conrad to princess Isabella. Manfred’s subjects notice his impatience. They suspect he arranged the marriage to avoid an ancient prophecy stating that his castle and rule of Otranto would pass from his family when the real owner became too large to inhabit it.

The wedding is set for Conrad’s birthday. On that day, Conrad is missing. A servant discovers in the courtyard that a giant helmet has fallen from the sky and crushed Conrad to death. Realizing his only male heir is dead and his wife cannot bear more children, Manfred decides to marry Isabella himself. He proposes this to Isabella. She refuses, and he seizes her, apparently intending to rape her. A series of supernatural events, including the ghost of his grandfather, distracts Manfred, and Isabella breaks free. She escapes toward the church of St. Nicholas with the help of a peasant named Theodore. Manfred is then confronted by his guards, who report seeing a giant armored leg in the gallery. Later, a group of knights arrives, searching for Isabella on behalf of her father, the Marquis of Vicenza.

Outside the castle, Theodore defends Isabella from a knight. He wounds the knight and discovers, with dismay, that the knight is Isabella’s father, Frederic. Theodore, Frederic, and Isabella return to the castle. Frederic recovers and explains to Manfred’s wife, Hippolita, how he came to Otranto: while at war, he had a vision warning that his daughter was in danger. The vision led him to a forest, where a hermit showed him a gigantic sword inscribed with a prophecy.

Manfred observes Theodore’s resemblance to the hero Alfonso and again tries to secure marriage to Isabella. He proposes to Frederic that they marry each other’s daughters. Frederic initially agrees but is haunted by the ghost of the hermit and decides not to proceed with the double marriage.

Manfred is furious, especially after learning that Theodore is meeting a woman in Alfonso’s tomb. Believing Isabella is having an affair with Theodore, Manfred enters the tomb and fatally stabs the woman. He realizes with horror that he has killed his own daughter, Matilda, not Isabella. Moments later, the castle wall behind Manfred crumbles, revealing a gigantic vision of Alfonso. The vision declares that his grandson Theodore is the true heir of Otranto. Manfred then reveals that his grandfather poisoned Alfonso and usurped the throne. To atone, Manfred agrees to abdicate. The novel ends with Frederic offering Isabella in marriage to Theodore. Theodore eventually agrees, but mourns the loss of his true love Matilda for many years.
### Characters
- **Manfred** – lord of the Castle of Otranto, father of Conrad and Matilda, husband of Hippolita. After Conrad is killed by a falling helmet, Manfred becomes obsessed with ending his marriage to Hippolita and pursuing Isabella, who was to marry Conrad. He is the antagonist of the novel. He is the ruler and father, and he is cruel to his children.
- **Hippolita** – wife of Manfred, mother of Conrad and Matilda. After Conrad's death, she and Matilda are left to face Manfred's tyranny. Manfred intends to divorce her because she is sterile and because he claims their marriage is invalid due to kinship. Faced with the threat of divorce, Hippolita is mournful and submissive to her husband's will. She enables her husband by setting aside her morals and happiness.
- **Conrad** – the fifteen-year-old son of Manfred and Hippolita and younger brother of Matilda. On his way to marry Isabella, he is crushed by a giant helmet.
- **Matilda** – daughter of Hippolita and Manfred. She falls in love with Theodore, a love her parents do not approve of. Frederic desires Matilda. Frederic and Manfred plan to marry each other's daughters, which prevents Matilda from being with Theodore. At the end of the novel, she is mistakenly stabbed by her father.
- **Isabella** – daughter of Frederic, fiancée of Conrad at the start of the novel. After Conrad's death, she states she would have preferred marriage to Conrad over marriage to his father, who pursues her. She and Matilda argue briefly because both have feelings for Theodore. After Matilda's death, Theodore marries Isabella, and they become lord and lady of the castle.
- **Theodore** – initially points out the helmet's link to the prophecy. Manfred orders him imprisoned within the helmet, but he escapes and helps Isabella flee through a trapdoor. He is later revealed to be the lost son of Friar Jerome. He protects Isabella from Manfred. Both Isabella and Matilda fall in love with him. After Matilda's death, he marries Isabella and goes on to rule the Castle of Otranto.
- **Friar Jerome** – friar at the monastery near the castle. Manfred attempts to manipulate him into supporting the plan to divorce Hippolita and into persuading Hippolita to agree. He is later discovered to be Theodore's father.
- **Frederic** – Isabella's father, who reappears late in the novel. He initially opposes Manfred, then agrees to marry Matilda as part of a deal.
- **Bianca** – Matilda's servant, who provides comic relief.
- **Diego** and **Jaquez** – other servants in the Castle of Otranto.
### Preface
Source: Horace Walpole, _The Castle of Otranto, A Gothic Story_, 2nd edn (London: William Bathoe and Thomas Lownds, 1765), pp. v–xi, xv–xvi
#### Preface to the First Edition (1764)
The following work was found in the library of an ancient Catholic family in the north of England. It was printed at Naples, in the black letter, in the year 1529. How much sooner it was written does not appear. The principal incidents are such as were believed in the darkest ages of christianity; but the language and conduct have nothing that savours of barbarism. The style is purest Italian. If the story was written near the time when it is supposed to have happened, it must have been between 1095, the era of the first crusade, and 1243, the date of the last, or not long afterwards. There is no other circumstance in the work, that can lead us to guess at the period in which the scene is laid. The names of the actors are evidently fictitious, and probably disguised on purpose: yet the Spanish names of the domestics seem to indicate that this work was not composed until the establishment of the Arragonian kings in Naples had made Spanish appellations familiar in that country. The beauty of the diction, and the zeal of the author (moderated, however, by singular judgment), concur to make me think, that the date of the composition was little antecedent to that of the impression. Letters were then in their most flourishing state in Italy, and contributed to dispel the empire of superstition, at that time so forcibly attacked by the reformers. . . .  
Miracles, visions, necromancy, dreams, and other preternatural events, are exploded now even from romances. That was not the case when our author wrote; much less when the story itself is supposed to have happened. Belief in every kind of prodigy was so established in those dark ages, that an author would not be faithful to the manners of the times, who should omit all mention of them. He is not bound to believe them himself, but he must represent his actors as believing them.  
If this air of the miraculous is excused, the reader will find nothing else unworthy of his perusal. Allow the possibility of the facts, and all the actors comport themselves as persons would do in their situation. There is no bombast, no similes, flowers, digressions, or unnecessary descriptions. Every thing tends directly to the catastrophe. Never is the reader’s attention relaxed. The rules of the drama are almost observed throughout the conduct of the piece. The characters are well drawn, and still better maintained. Terror, the author’s principal engine, prevents the story from ever languishing; and it is so often contrasted by pity, that the mind is kept up in a constant vicissitude of interesting passions.  
Some persons may, perhaps, think the characters of the domestics too little serious for the general cast of the story; but, besides their opposition to the principal personages, the art of the author is very observable in his conduct of the subalterns. They discover many passages essential to the story, which could not be well brought to light but by their naivetè-sic and simplicity: in particular, the womanish terror and foibles of Bianca, in the last chapter, conduce essentially towards advancing the catastrophe. . . .
#### Preface to the Second Edition (1765)
The favourable manner in which this little piece has been received by the public, calls upon the author to explain the grounds on which he composed it. But before he opens those motives, it is fit that he should ask pardon of his readers for having offered his work to them under the borrowed personage of a translator. As diffidence of his own abilities, and the novelty of the attempt, were the sole inducements to assume that disguise, he flatters himself he shall appear excusable. He resigned his performance to the impartial judgment of the public; determined to let it perish in obscurity, if disapproved; nor meaning to avow such a trifle, unless better judges should pronounce that he might own it without a blush.  
It was an attempt to blend the two kinds of Romance, the ancient and the modern. In the former, all was imagination and improbability: in the latter, nature is always intended to be, and sometimes has been, copied with success. Invention has not been wanting; but the great resources of fancy have been dammed up, by a strict adherence to common life. But if in the latter species Nature has cramped imagination, she did but take her revenge, having been totally excluded from old Romances. The actions, sentiments, conversations, of the heroes and heroines of ancient days were as unnatural as the machines employed to put them in motion.  
The author of the following pages thought it possible to reconcile the two kinds. Desirous of leaving the powers of fancy at liberty to expatiate through the boundless realms of invention, and thence of creating more interesting situations, he wished to conduct the mortal agents in his drama according to the rules of probability; in short, to make them think, speak and act, as it might be supposed mere men and women would do in extraordinary positions. He had observed, that in all inspired writings, the personages under the dispensation of miracles and witnesses of the most stupendous phenomena, never lose sight of their human character: whereas in the productions of romantic story, an improbable event never fails to be attended by an absurd dialogue. The actors seem to lose their senses, the moment the laws of nature have lost their tone. As the public have applauded the attempt, the author must not say he was entirely unequal to the task he had undertaken: yet if the new route he has struck out shall have paved a road for men of brighter talents, he shall own with pleasure and modesty, that he was sensible the plan was capable of receiving greater embellishments than his imagination or conduct of the passions could bestow on it.  
With regard to the deportment of the domestics, on which I have touched in the former preface, I will beg leave to add a few words. The simplicity of their behaviour, almost tending to excite smiles, which at first seems not consonant to the serious cast of the work, appeared to me not only not improper, but was marked designedly in that manner. My rule was Nature. However grave, important, or even melancholy, the sensations of Princes and heroes may be, they do not stamp the same affections on their domestics: at least the latter do not, or should not be made to express their passions in the same dignified tone. In my humble opinion, the contrast between the sublime of the one and the _naivete_ of the others, sets the pathetic of the former in a stronger light. The very impatience which a reader feels, while delayed by the coarse pleasantries of vulgar actors from arriving at the knowledge of the important catastrophe he expects, perhaps heightens, certainly proves, that he has been artfully interested in the depending event. But I had higher authority than my own opinion for this conduct. The great master of nature, SHAKESPEARE, was the model I copied. Let me ask, if his tragedies of _Hamlet_ and _Julius Caesar_ would not lose a considerable share of their spirit and wonderful beauties, if the humour of the grave-diggers, the fooleries of Polonius, and the clumsy jests of the Roman citizens, were omitted, or vested in heroics? Is not the eloquence of _Antony_, the nobler and affectedly-unaffected oration of _Brutus_, artificially exalted by the rude bursts of nature from the mouths of their auditors? These touches remind one of the _Grecian_ sculptor, who to convey the idea of a Colossus within the dimensions of a seal, inserted a little boy measuring his thumb.  
No, says _Voltaire_ in his edition of _Corneille_, this mixture of buffoonery and solemnity is intolerable. – _Voltaire_ is a genius – but not of _Shakespeare_’s magnitude. Without recurring to disputable authority, I appeal from _Voltaire_ to himself. I shall not availl myself of his former encomiums on our mighty poet; though the _French_ critic has twice translated the same speech in _Hamlet_, some years ago in admiration, latterly in derision; and I am sorry to find that his judgment grows weaker when it ought to be farther matured. . . .  
The result of all I have said, is, to shelter my own daring under the canon of the brightest genius this country, at least, has produced. I might have pleaded, that having created a new species of romance, I was at liberty to lay down what rules I thought fit for the conduct of it: But I should be more proud of having imitated, however faintly, weakly, and at a distance, so masterly a pattern, than to enjoy the entire merit of invention, unless I could have marked my work with genius as well as with originality. Such as it is, the Public have honoured it sufficiently, whatever rank their suffrages allot to it.
## Why It’s Here
It is generally regarded as the first Gothic novel.
## Historical & Literary Context
### Authorial Context
Horace Walpole was fascinated with medieval history and artefacts. In 1749 he began construction on his own Gothic castle, Strawberry Hill House. Strawberry Hill was one of the first examples of Gothic Revival architecture; it appealed to the baroque tastes of the time and later became entwined with the Gothic literature movement.

Walpole wrote _The Castle of Otranto_ in 1764 during his tenure as MP for King's Lynn. The first edition was titled _The Castle of Otranto, A Story. Translated by William Marshal, Gent. From the Original Italian of Onuphrio Muralto, Canon of the Church of St. Nicholas at Otranto_. It claimed to be a translation of a manuscript written at Naples in 1529 and recently rediscovered in the library of "an ancient Catholic family in the north of England". The story was said to derive from a still older tale dating back to the Crusades (between 1095 and 1243). Walpole used an archaic writing style to reinforce this pretence. The Italian manuscript and its author "Onuphrio Muralto" were fictional creations, and "William Marshal" was Walpole's pseudonym. Some early reviewers accepted the work as medieval fiction and praised the "ingenious translator".

In the second and subsequent editions Walpole admitted authorship. He explained that he had attempted to blend two kinds of romance: ancient (all imagination and improbability) and modern (in which nature is copied). In this blending he placed ordinary people in extraordinary situations. At the time there was debate over whether fiction should represent life or be purely imaginative. After Walpole acknowledged the work as his own, many critics dismissed it as absurd, fluffy, romantic fiction, or as unsavory or immoral.
### Public Reaction
The novel was a commercial success and inspired imitators. Clara Reeve published _The Old English Baron_ in 1778; in her preface she wrote: "This Story is the literary offspring of The Castle of Otranto, written upon the same plan, with a design to unite the most attractive and interesting circumstances of the ancient Romance and modern Novel."

Gothic literature was a matter of concern for writers and critics in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries because it was a very popular genre, especially among women readers. In 1773, John and Letitia Aikin (later Letitia Barbauld) wrote in an essay that tales of ghosts, murders, and disasters were eagerly consumed by the public. The growing literate population continued to demand the kind of fiction Walpole had started. In 1797, an anonymous critic in _The Spirit of the Public Journals_ stated that making terror the order of the day had become fashionable. The Gothic appealed to both the leisure and working classes. This cross-class appeal provoked a backlash from elite conservative critics who wanted to preserve the cultural capital associated with literacy. The backlash took many forms.

A common accusation was that Gothic romances were immoral, corrupted readers’ minds, and encouraged social disorder. The pedagogical effect on children was considered especially dangerous. An anonymous critic in _The Spirit of the Public Journals_ in 1797 argued that Gothic fantasies defied common sense, gave bad moral lessons, and hurt young readers’ imaginations. The critic contrasted Gothic romances with “useful” novels that depict real life and correct follies, and described Gothic content as ideas from lunatics. The same critic stated that these texts at best teach “Thou shalt do no murder” and at worst teach dissociation from everyday life. The critic believed Gothic romances would ruin young people’s capacity for labour and public service. In 1796, the _Monthly Review_ called the taste for the marvellous and the terrible a plague or infection that must be stamped out for the health of the nation.

The perceived effect on women was considered even more frightening. Gothic fiction was accused of producing mannish women who would prefer death-defying adventures and thrilling romances over settling into marriage and domestic duties. The critic in _The Spirit of the Public Journals_ asked whether women’s bodies were so hardy that they needed softening by contact with dead bodies and damp sweats, and argued that young ladies could be taught nothing more necessary than sleeping in dungeons with reptiles, walking with assassins, and carrying bloody daggers instead of pin-cushions and needle-books. Conservative critics believed a whole generation of women—future wives and mothers—was in danger of contamination. They argued that Gothic books threatened matrimony by training women to imagine weddings as dramatic spectacles featuring long dangerous galleries, blue-burning lights, thunder, and a murdered man’s visage at a window, rather than as a solemn union before Church and State. Conservatives feared social decay if women preferred Gothic fantasies of confronting ghouls and nightmares with a dashing young man over marrying a gentleman selected by their parents. The Gothic emphasized power relations, entanglements, veiling, and entrapment, and taught women to suspect husbands, fathers, and priests of harbouring malevolent intentions.

A recurring criticism was that Gothic novels were predictable and artless, employing formulaic elements and generic character names like Julia or Jemima. _The Spirit of the Public Journals_ provided a satirical description of the Gothic formula (e.g., a curtain withdrawn reveals a bleeding body, a chest opened contains a skeleton) and a “recipe” for a Gothic novel that included an old castle, secret doors, bodies, skeletons, assassins, and groans. The satire implied that anyone could write a successful Gothic romance, that authors were manufacturers rather than artists, and that the genre was a moneymaking conspiracy between booksellers, printers, and hack writers.

The widespread availability of Gothic romances was enabled by subscription-based circulating libraries, which began appearing in England around the mid-eighteenth century. According to John Feather, the Gothic craze was sustained by library demand. Libraries loaned duodecimo books, stocking commercially successful fiction—primarily romances and Gothic novels—rather than devotional or instructive literature. These institutions helped move fiction from the aristocracy into the mainstream and made literature increasingly commercial.

Circulating libraries drew criticism from the economic and cultural elite. In _Biographia Literaria_, Samuel Taylor Coleridge stated that reading Gothic novels from circulating libraries was not reading but a kind of beggarly daydreaming that produced laziness and mawkish sensibility. He compared it to idle pastimes such as gaming, swinging on a chair, spitting over a bridge, smoking, snuff-taking, and post-dinner quarrels between husband and wife. In the preface to the second edition of _Lyrical Ballads_, William Wordsworth referred to Gothic novels as “frantic novels, sickly and stupid German tragedies.” He claimed that they numbed sympathy and imagination, created a degrading thirst after outrageous stimulation, used gross and violent stimulants that obscured reality, and were characterized by gaudiness and inane phraseology. Wordsworth theorized his and Coleridge’s poetic practice—using simple language to meditate in tranquility on the emotions of ordinary rustic people in nature—as the opposite of the Gothic, presenting Romantic poetry as a way to wean society off Gothic addiction.

Despite these attacks, the early Romantics made use of Gothic tropes. In the first edition of _Lyrical Ballads_, Coleridge’s [[The Rime of the Ancient Mariner - ST Coleridge Gustave Dor]] (which includes supernatural elements, ghosts, monsters, and curses) was placed as the opening poem. Coleridge also wrote the Gothic poem [[Christabel (Classic Reprint) - Samuel Taylor Coleridge]] between 1797 and 1800. Wordsworth wrote poems such as “We Are Seven,” “Lucy Gray,” The Ruined Cottage, and the “Lucy” poems, which focused on death and haunting. The second generation of Romantics—including Lord Byron, Thomas De Quincey, John Polidori, John Keats, and Mary and Percy Shelley—openly embraced the Gothic. Byron wrote [[Manfred - George Gordon Byron]], named after the antagonist of The Castle of Otranto, and Mary Shelley wrote [[Frankenstein The 1818 Text - Mary Shelley]]
